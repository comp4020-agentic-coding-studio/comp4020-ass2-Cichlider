Self-report is the least reliable monitoring channel a relationship has, not
because people lie often, but because self-report is cheap to produce and
mostly reflects how someone wants to be seen answering, not the underlying
state. "Everything's fine" is nearly free to say and carries almost no
information. The model instead monitors behavioural signals that are costly
to fake because faking them costs something real, and it prices seven of
them, not one.

Three are about who does the work: initiation rate (who starts the plans),
disclosure rate (who volunteers information without being asked), and
reciprocity balance (whether that effort stays roughly symmetric over a long
window, not just this week). Two are about whether a signal can be trusted at
all: its cost (did producing it consume real time, money or opportunity, or
was it free to say) and its specificity (was it directed at you, or is it
generic courtesy that would have gone to anyone). The last two are about
durability: persistence (does warmth survive past the novelty and
convenience period, or does it only show up when nothing else is competing
for the same hour) and repair speed (how quickly a conflict gets addressed
once it surfaces). All seven degrade quietly, well before self-report
catches up, which is why "monitoring" is a separate skill from "checking in."

## Outline

- why self-report is a weak monitoring channel, and what makes a signal
  costly to fake
- seven behavioural signals, in three groups: effort (initiation rate,
  disclosure rate, reciprocity balance), trustworthiness (cost, specificity),
  durability (persistence, repair speed)
- reading a signal trend, not a signal snapshot — one bad week is noise,
  four is a trend
- reading for next week: what to do once a monitored signal actually
  crosses a threshold
